Demo script

Walking a professor through SecurityPolicy AI

Each section says what to show, where to click and what to say. Activity data (campaigns, quiz scores) is simulated demo data and labelled as such; the content was really generated by the system.

01 · 1 min

The problem

Security-awareness training in most companies is stale (written once, reused for years), inconsistent (different authors repeat or contradict each other) and generic (every department gets the same lesson, although they make different mistakes).

Attackers change tactics every month, but training content doesn't. And Finance falls for fake invoices while HR falls for fake CVs, yet both get the same slides.
02 · 1 min

Our solution

An assistant that writes awareness material which is current (it reads official guidance and news daily), consistent (it remembers what was taught), targeted (it knows each team's real mistakes) and trustworthy (every fact is cited and fact-checked before a human approves it).

The brief asked for Firecrawl for current guidance, Memento for memory and an LLM to write. We built that, and we closed the loop: phishing tests and quizzes feed back in, so the next lesson targets what people actually got wrong.
03 · 2 min

How it works

01 COLLECTTrusted sourcesFirecrawl reads 16 sites (CISA, NCSC, CERT-In, FBI…) + daily news feeds + CISA exploited-vulnerability list.
02 STOREKnowledge baseText split into ~1,000 passages, searchable by meaning (ChromaDB, local embeddings).
03 REMEMBERMemoryMemento keeps approved lessons, style rules and each department's recurring mistakes.
04 WRITEGeminiGets the best sources + the team's mistakes + past lessons; must cite every fact.
05 CHECKJudge + humanA second AI checks each claim against its source; a human approves.

Mistake data is real: APT-Hunter scans Windows event logs for risky behaviour, and the NEST-Phish dataset records how real people answered phishing tests. Both are mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques.

This is retrieval-augmented generation: the model never writes from memory alone. It writes from the passages we hand it, and has to cite them.

05 · 3 min

Create Open

Overview → Teach on a Finance risk (or Create → topic "invoice fraud", audience Finance, Newsletter) → Generate

Choose a topic, one of six formats (newsletter, tip, quiz, poster, phishing simulation, policy summary), an audience and a language (English, Hindi, Telugu). While it works, the checklist shows each stage. The draft opens with three tabs:

  • Content: orange [n] markers are citations; click one to jump to the source. "org" marks facts from our own telemetry.
  • Sources: the exact trusted pages used, with dates.
  • Review: the judge's scores (facts supported, claims cited, on target, style, clarity) and any claim to double-check.

Approve saves it to memory, so future content builds on it and avoids repeating it. Export to Word or Markdown. Links to untrusted sites block approval.

The judge is a separate AI call that checks every claim against the cited passage. It's not perfect, so a human still approves, but it tells the reviewer exactly where to look.

Tip: generation takes 20–90 s. To avoid waiting, open an existing draft from the Library instead.

06 · 1 min

Library Open

Library → filter Approved → open the Finance quiz, then the policy summary (a draft)

Every piece of content, searchable and filterable. Update suggested appears when a lesson is old or newer guidance on its topic has arrived; the feeds refresh automatically every day.

This is the "keep it current" part of the brief: the system notices when its own lessons go out of date.
07 · 2 min

Phishing simulation Open

Simulate → look at the Finance campaigns (click rate falling week by week) → Launch a new one with 3 recipients → open a recipient's inbox link → click the link → sign in with fake text → see the debrief

No real email is sent. Each recipient gets a private local inbox page. Opens, clicks, typed data (never stored) and reports are recorded. A click rate of 25%+ becomes a recurring mistake for that team, and new content targets it.

This closes the loop: we don't just teach, we measure whether people actually changed behaviour, and feed that back into what we teach next.
08 · 1 min

Insights Open

Insights → Finance → trend chart → This week tab → Threat feed digest tab

Per-department recurring mistakes with their source (logs, NEST-Phish, quizzes, simulations), the weekly trend of click rate and quiz score, team totals for the week (never individual rankings), and a digest that proposes new topics from the last 14 days of news.

In the demo data, Finance's click rate falls from 50% to 17% over four weeks while quiz scores rise. That's the evidence the training works.

Limitations and ethics

  • The organisation "Nebulon" and its departments are simulated; the mistake data is real (one Windows host, NEST-Phish participants), so per-department rates are noisy.
  • Campaign and quiz history in this demo is simulated and tagged "demo".
  • The judge is an AI, so scores are indicative; a human approves everything.
  • Free-tier quotas limit throughput; we rotate keys and cache responses.
  • Simulations only use example.com addresses, never store typed credentials, and should only run with staff consent.

Likely questions

How do you stop the AI making things up?
It only gets trusted passages, must cite them, a judge checks each claim, untrusted links are blocked, and a human approves.
What does Memento add over the vector database?
The vector DB holds outside knowledge; Memento holds organisational memory: what we taught, when, style rules and recurring mistakes. It stops repetition and keeps the tone consistent.
Why is the mistake data trustworthy?
It comes from real Windows event logs (APT-Hunter) and real human phishing-test answers (NEST-Phish), mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques.
How is "current" guaranteed?
Feeds refresh every 24 h, retrieval prefers recent sources, and the Library flags lessons overtaken by newer guidance.
How did you evaluate it?
An ablation study (A–D) with an LLM judge for factuality, citation coverage, relevance, style and clarity, plus computed freshness and novelty.
What about privacy?
Pseudonymous IDs, no stored credentials, team-level reporting only, and API keys and raw logs never leave the machine.
Could it run in a real company?
Yes: replace the simulated org file with real departments, point APT-Hunter at real logs, and connect the simulation to the company mail system.
